Lore Foundation

Chronos, The Unspent Hour

Scales with the advancing round, may freeze the Undead action that would kill him, and can force a Seventh Round when time should have ended.

Unit Role

Custodian of the Seventh Hour

The sixth bell marks the end only for those who believe the clock belongs to them.

There is an hour that does not belong to any day. Chronos keeps it folded inside his chest. No scripture agrees on his origin. The angelic archives describe a light seen moving before the first dawn, as though something had arrived early to witness creation. Hell's oldest wall bears a shadow that the masons insist was present before the wall. The Gatekeeper's ledgers contain several judgements signed by Chronos in dates separated by millennia and written in the same unworn hand. All accounts may be true. Chronos does not correct them. To explain a beginning, he says, is merely to choose where the listener entered the story.

He is not the god of time, because gods invite worship and Chronos has no use for it. He is time made responsible for its own continuity: custodian of sequence, keeper of the distance between cause and consequence, witness to the fact that an ending must arrive after what it ends. His domain is the Meridian, a place visible from neither Living nor Dead Realm except at moments of extreme delay. There, abandoned possibilities hang like pendulums. Snow falls upward into unborn storms. Bells wait with their sound still inside them.

Chronos wears no age consistently. The face beneath his mantle may appear young while the hands are mapped with centuries, or ancient until he turns and is briefly seen as the child he has not yet been.

Constellations move through the fabric around him. The mechanisms at his belt are not clocks but reliquaries, each holding a duration removed from history: the second before a king gave an order, the winter a city survived, the breath an assassin never took.

He does not command life or death. Both are events, and events fall within sequence. This places him beside the other Commanders without making him their kin. Archimedes asks time to heal. Balthazar asks it to compound. The Gatekeeper asks it to wait while a soul is judged. Chronos grants none of these requests from affection. He preserves the conditions under which requests, decisions and consequences can remain distinct.

The single moment in which he protects himself is called the Unspent Hour. When an Undead action would end his Living form, Chronos may refuse the event its place in sequence. The blow does not miss. It simply has not happened. Weapons return to the posture before impact; commands retreat into the mouths that issued them; wounds close without ever having existed. Only Chronos remembers the stolen turn, and even he pays for it. A silver crack appears somewhere upon his body, marking a future that now knows it was denied.

When Chronos enters the Dead Realm, he steps outside the interval in which death could be meaningfully completed. His colours become cosmic and remote; his outline carries after-images of earlier and later positions. Unlike the Gatekeeper, he does not cross back. Once time has withdrawn from ordinary sequence, the battle must conclude around that absence.

His cards and attendants grow more potent as the rounds advance because Chronos has never regarded early and late as equal conditions. Patience gathers weight. Prophecy narrows. A small act in the first round may return enormous in the sixth because consequence has had time to mature. Some of his powers can make a round repeat - not by reversing history, but by insisting that the chosen interval has not yet fulfilled its meaning. Whether the terrain remains or is rolled anew determines if time repeats the same circumstance or merely the same opportunity.

The Seventh Round is his most closely guarded office. Six rounds define the accepted span of a Deathmancer contest; the seventh exists outside that covenant, an emergency hour released only when neither side has achieved dominion. Chronos does not create it. He opens the reliquary in his chest and returns to the world the time it assumed it had spent. Bells strike an impossible number. The dying stand long enough for one final choice. Engines believed complete discover they have another turn in which to fail.

This power is not benevolent. Additional time does not favour the good; it favours whatever has prepared to survive into it. Chronos knows that delay can save a city or prolong a torture, permit redemption or give corruption another season to root. His apparent calm is not indifference but the fatigue of one who has witnessed every virtue beg for more time and every monster make the same request.

He speaks rarely of destiny. To Chronos, destiny is only a name used by those who encounter the consequence after forgetting the cause. His purpose is not to decide who wins, but to ensure the ending has truly earned the right to arrive.
